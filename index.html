01

Why the Learning Rate Matters Most

The gradient gives a direction; the learning rate \(\eta\) decides the step. Too small and the model crawls; too large and the steps overshoot. In between is a band where it trains well.

03

Warmup

$$\eta_t = \eta\,\frac{t+1}{W} \qquad (t < W)$$

Most runs start with a few hundred to a few thousand steps of linearly rising learning rate. Early on the gradients are large and change fast, and Adam’s running averages are built from only a few steps, so a full-size first step can throw the weights somewhere bad. Warmup was popularised for large-batch training by Goyal et al. (2017) and is essential for post-LN transformers (Xiong et al. 2020; deck 06).

Measured: this model doesn’t need it

This tiny pre-LN model at a peak of 0.01 trains fine without warmup: the run that starts at the full rate is at 2.382 at step 1,000 against 2.640 with 50 warmup steps, and ends at 2.159 ± 0.045 against 2.246 ± 0.025. Warmup is insurance that this model happens not to need.

04

Decay: Cosine and Warmup–Stable–Decay

At the end of a run a smaller learning rate lets the weights settle into a valley instead of bouncing across it. The cosine schedule (Loshchilov & Hutter 2016) decays smoothly from the start; LLaMA used it down to 10% of the peak (Touvron et al. 2023, §2.3). Its drawback: the run’s length must be fixed in advance. WSD (Hu et al. 2024, studied by Hägele et al. 2024) holds the peak and decays only at the end, so a run can be extended from any checkpoint of the stable phase.

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta - \eta_{\min})\Big(1 + \cos \tfrac{\pi (t-W)}{T-W}\Big)$$

The cosine run ends where it does because a 2,000-step run that is still improving at its end gains little from spending its last steps at a small learning rate. Decay pays off in long runs that are close to converged; this toy isn’t, and the spreads overlap.

06

Batch Size and the Critical Batch

Each step’s gradient is an average over the batch: a noisy estimate of the true gradient. A bigger batch gives a better estimate, so the same progress needs fewer steps, but each step costs more. McCandlish et al. (2018) model the trade-off: doubling the batch halves the steps well below a critical batch size, and barely helps above it.

$$S = S_{\min}\Big(1 + \frac{B_{\text{crit}}}{B}\Big)$$
Each point at the better of two learning rates, three seeds (results.md §6c). Fit RMS error 0.077 in log steps.

Steps to a validation loss of 2.6 at five batch sizes, each at the better of two learning rates, three seeds (results.md §6c). From 4 to 64 windows the steps fall from 1,950 to 625, far less than the 16-fold the batch grew. The fit gives \(B_{\text{crit}}\) = 10.4 windows, \(S_{\min}\) = 592 steps.

The Gradient Noise Scale

McCandlish et al.’s quick estimate of the critical batch is the gradient noise scale: the gradient’s variance over its squared size (their eq. 2.9), the batch at which noise is as large as signal.

The trend is the useful part: as the loss falls, the gradient’s signal shrinks relative to its noise, so later in training a bigger batch is worth more, which is why some large runs grow their batch during training.

08

The Linear Scaling Rule

The linear scaling rule (Goyal et al. 2017): when you multiply the batch by \(k\), multiply the learning rate by \(k\) too, up to a limit, since \(k\) small steps in about the same direction add up to one \(k\)-times larger step.

Where it holds

SGD, below the critical batch, after warmup (Goyal et al. trained ImageNet with large batches this way, with a warmup).

Where it weakens

Adam’s normalised step: the step size no longer scales with the gradient. Large language-model runs therefore tune the batch size and learning rate together, and the measured best rate here changed between batch sizes (results.md §6c).

Batch size is also where parallelism meets optimisation: more data-parallel workers means a bigger batch per step (deck 10), and the critical batch says how far that helps.

09

What to Take Away

  • The learning rate is the hyperparameter: find its stable band with a sweep, a factor of about 3 per rung, several seeds.
  • Tune it per method: anything that changes what the optimiser sees changes the rate it wants.
  • Warmup is insurance; decay (cosine, or WSD so the run can be extended) pays off near convergence.
  • Bigger batches cut steps until the critical batch; the gradient noise scale estimates it and grows as training goes on.
